\title{Should We Search for a Better Agent Configuration?\\
\large Sequential Efficacy, Futility, and the Cost of Choosing the Best of Many}
\author{Sanjay Mukhyala \and Ian Waudby-Smith}
\date{Working manuscript --- October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
Evaluating many prompts or agent configurations against an incumbent creates two problems: an apparent winner may be selected by noise, and a fixed evaluation budget may be spent after the answer is already clear. We study an incumbent-relative sequential protocol with distinct decisions for efficacy (some challenger improves on the incumbent), practical futility (no challenger improves by a prespecified margin), and insufficient evidence. We apply it to prospective web-agent studies and registered historical replays of prompt and agent benchmarks. In PromptEval, the best of 99 prompts leads a positional reference by 5.02 percentage points on search examples but by 0.54 points held out; 421 of 855 settings certify 15-point futility and only four cross efficacy. In a separate paraphrase benchmark with an original-template incumbent, the selected prompt improves by 15.04 points held out, although its search-half lead overstates that gain by 7.92 points; 58 of 305 settings cross efficacy. Two fixed SWE-bench system comparisons cross efficacy after 92 and 95 paired issues, whereas an 8.8-point Terminal-Bench scaffold gain does not cross within 68 tasks. Live rerun checks are compatible with nominal efficacy calibration, while a measured 15-point default shift reverses a historical-bank comparison. These heterogeneous outcomes motivate a protocol that reports unresolved searches and contemporaneous controls. A pilot-based predictor fails cross-dataset validation, so our design guidance gives exact evaluation costs without promising universal stopping times.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
An agent designer commonly has an incumbent configuration and a pool of candidate prompts, scaffolds, or complete systems. A fixed-grid comparison evaluates all candidates on all tasks and chooses the largest observed mean. The choice is sensitive to the number of candidates: searching more rows raises the largest noisy score, even if no candidate is better. Evaluating every row to a fixed horizon also uses resources after a large gain or a lack of practically useful gain becomes statistically clear.

We ask two separate questions. \emph{Efficacy}: is there evidence that at least one candidate beats the incumbent? \emph{Futility}: is there evidence that every candidate improves by less than a chosen meaningful margin $\delta$? A positive answer to neither is a substantive outcome: the available tasks do not resolve the search. These questions resemble multi-arm trial monitoring and can be addressed by betting-based e-processes, which allow monitoring at every task under their sampling assumptions~\citep{waudbysmith2024betting,ramdas2023gametheoretic}. Candidate generation remains unrestricted; automatic prompt optimizers provide many ways to propose rows~\citep{zhou2023ape,yang2024opro,agrawal2025gepa}.

Our contribution is an empirical account of what these decisions look like across several kinds of pools. The live web-agent studies give prospective, preregistered uses of the protocol. Public matrices give breadth across models, tasks, and candidate counts, with protocols committed before their outcomes were analysed. They are historical replays of complete benchmark results, so their stopping-time savings are counterfactual and their nominal thresholds require a fresh-task interpretation. The contrast between a positional PromptEval reference and an original-template paraphrase incumbent is central: the baseline defines both the question and the apparent prevalence of useful discoveries.

\section{Incumbent-relative sequential decisions}
\label{sec:method}
Let $b$ denote a fixed incumbent and $k=1,\ldots,K$ fixed challengers. On paired task $t$, observe bounded outcomes $Y_{bt},Y_{kt}\in[0,1]$ and set $D_{kt}=Y_{kt}-Y_{bt}$. The target gain is $\Delta_k=\mathbb{E}D_{kt}$ for a prespecified task population. The efficacy null is $H^+_0:\Delta_k\leq0$ for every $k$; the pool futility null is $H^-_0:\Delta_k\geq\delta$ for at least one $k$. At $\delta>0$, these hypotheses are not complements. A pool can contain a small real improvement that is also below the meaningful margin.

For binary paired outcomes, the efficacy process for challenger $k$ bets on its result on discordant tasks. Under the null and the corresponding conditional task-sampling assumption, a challenger win is no more likely than an incumbent win among discordances. A predictable one-sided betting fraction $\lambda_{kt}\geq0$ produces a nonnegative capital process $E^+_{kt}$. We use the existing hedged-capital update in the repository and combine challengers by an equal mixture,
\begin{equation}
 E^+_t=K^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^K E^+_{kt}.
\end{equation}
Under the global efficacy null this mixture starts at one and controls the chance of ever crossing $1/\alpha$. It tests existence of an improving arm; it does not identify the best arm or validate the argmax selected on the same examples.

For futility, transform each difference to $X_{kt}=(D_{kt}+1)/2$ and the margin to $m_\delta=(1+\delta)/2$. Under the single-arm null $\Delta_k\geq\delta$, downward factors $1-\lambda_{kt}(X_{kt}-m_\delta)$ form a nonnegative e-process for admissible predictable bets. The implementation mixes an adaptive bet with four fixed bets. We declare pool futility only when \emph{every} challenger process exceeds $1/\alpha$ at the same task. If any arm truly reaches $\delta$, its own process bounds a false pool-futility declaration. For both directions we report the first crossing and treat no crossing as unresolved. The two directional tests each use level $\alpha=0.05$ unless a replay specifies a pairwise correction; this is not a claim that every possible statement across all settings has joint 5\% error.

A full paired grid consumes $(K+1)N$ arm-task episodes for $N$ tasks. Monitoring can shorten it to $(K+1)\tau$ when a decision occurs at $\tau<N$. Dropping individual futility-certified challengers may reduce episodes further, but requires an implementation that respects what is observed at each step. The cost and inferential claims assume the incumbent and candidates are fixed independently of the monitored outcomes, tasks represent the intended population, and the required conditional calibration holds. Reusing a public benchmark, tuning candidates against it, or shifting the default between sessions weakens those interpretations.

\section{Studies and registered replays}
\label{sec:studies}
The prospective studies evaluated prompt pools for a web agent on Gmail and GitLab tasks, including structured, free-form, and manual-derived prompts. Their plans were recorded before paid episodes. They found no incumbent-relative efficacy crossing in the measured real pools; a Shopping pool reached 15-point futility around 64 tasks and a GitLab leg at task 65. The live studies therefore demonstrate an unresolved or futile search as well as practical complications of control coverage and session timing. A planted task-leaking positive control crossed rapidly, but its rerun gain was only 5.5 points, so it is not evidence that ordinary prompt search found a transferable winner.

The public analyses were registered before each source's relevant outcomes were inspected. PromptEval\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/PromptEval/PromptEval_MMLU_correctness}} supplies 855 model-by-subject matrices, each with 100 prompt rows and at least 100 examples. Its row 0 was fixed by position, with 99 challengers; its provenance as a deployment default is unknown. Mizrahi et al.'s released paraphrases\footnote{\url{https://github.com/SLAB-NLP/Multi-Prompt-LLM-Evaluation}} supply an original benchmark template flag and per-example automatic validation for open-source models. We selected the smallest-index original template, kept only paraphrases annotated correct, and required agreement between reconstructed itemwise accuracies and published prompt-level accuracies. This yielded 305 settings across 24 tasks and 16 models; four mismatched settings and a 26-example task were excluded under the registered rules. Both prompt datasets used disjoint search and held-out example halves, shared across models within a task.

Terminal-Bench\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/datasets/harborframework/terminal-bench-2-leaderboard}} supplied repeated outcomes for a same-model scaffold comparison on 68 complete tasks, as well as an A/A label-swap replay. SWE-bench Verified\footnote{\url{https://github.com/SWE-bench/experiments}} supplied two prespecified same-date, same-model \emph{whole-system} comparisons on roughly 500 common issues each. The compared systems had different attempt policies, so these contrasts do not isolate a scaffold effect. For each public matrix, the crossing time is computed from historical outcomes in a registered task order and checked where specified against 2,000 task-order replays. Neither replay recovers a genuinely prospective decision on unseen future tasks.

In PromptEval, 97.3\% of search halves show a lead, yet only 48.3\% of selected rows have a strictly positive held-out gain. Across the Mizrahi settings, 78.0\% of selected paraphrases have a positive held-out gain. The difference is evidence against a universal flat-pool story. The Mizrahi original template offers substantial room for some paraphrases to improve it, while broad search still exaggerates the selected gain. A search-half argmax and an efficacy crossing are different objects: a crossing establishes evidence for some candidate under its null, not an independent guarantee for the selected row.

The PromptEval search half has a median 86 examples and many futility decisions; dropping futility-certified rows uses 59.1\% fewer arm-example evaluations than its full grid in a separate replay. In Mizrahi, the search half has 50 examples, and 60 of 305 settings reach a first boundary. Their median crossing is 35.5 tasks; first-boundary stopping reduces full-grid evaluations by 6.6\%. Most settings remain unresolved. A shorter horizon and larger observed gains favor different boundaries, and neither dataset establishes a universal futility-to-efficacy cost ratio.

Large whole-system gains can be detected early. On matched SWE-bench issues, the GPT-4 comparison gains 20.0 points and crosses the pair-adjusted efficacy threshold after 92 of 495 issues; the Claude 3 Opus comparison gains 8.9 points and crosses after 95 of 496 in the fixed order. Both cross in all 2,000 task-order replays, although crossing times vary. In Terminal-Bench, a same-model Capy scaffold gains 8.8 paired points over its reference on 68 tasks but crosses in none of 2,000 orders. The latter is an observed power limit, not evidence of no gain. Across public matrices, benchmark reuse and past system development on the same task sets limit the population interpretation of a nominal sequential error threshold.

\subsection{Real repeats and a drifting default}
On four scorings of live same-configuration repeat cells, efficacy crossings in 2,000 conditional label-swap/order replays were 0.6\%, 2.15\%, 0.75\%, and 1.9\%, each below 5\%. The GitLab scorings reuse episodes and are not independent replications. Repeats are sparse per prompt; this is a diagnostic on observed noise rather than an estimate of future-session error.

The Gmail default moved from 71.7\% in an older 30-task session to 86.7\% in a later session on matched tasks. A current answer-key arm scored 73.3\%: its apparent gain is $+1.7$ points against the historical plug-in bank and $-13.3$ against the concurrent default. No registered efficacy process crossed on these 30 tasks, so the observed result is estimate bias and sign reversal, not a demonstrated false rejection. A confidence adjustment for estimation error in the old bank cannot, by itself, cover an unbounded change in task success probabilities. A concurrent incumbent or a prospectively justified drift bound is required for a cross-session known-baseline claim.

\section{Design guidance and limits}
\label{sec:discussion}
A design starts by naming the incumbent, target task population, meaningful margin $\delta$, candidate pool, and maximum paired task count. The full-grid episode budget is exactly $(K+1)N$; for 50 challengers and 80 tasks it is 4,080 episodes. Pilot observations can inform planning, but the predictive model we tested did not transfer in both directions between PromptEval and Mizrahi. It improved multiclass Brier and log loss on held-out tasks within a mixed dataset, yet a PromptEval-trained model worsened Brier when applied to Mizrahi. At a 50-task horizon PromptEval supplied no efficacy decisions for that transfer fit, whereas Mizrahi supplied 58. We therefore do not offer a validated general tasks-to-decision calculator or promise a fixed episode-saving percentage.

For practice, a concurrent incumbent on every sampled task is the cleanest comparison when model behavior or task conditions may drift. A small pilot can reveal discordance and rough effect sizes, but a weak pilot should lead to a larger task budget or an unresolved result, not a forced winner. A futility margin should express a gain worth deploying, since the procedure can reasonably find evidence of a small improvement and evidence that every improvement is below the chosen margin. If a public fixed benchmark is used, results should be described as historical replay evidence and checked on new tasks before deployment.

The study has several limits. The live web-agent evidence comes from one model and a small number of applications; its controls and repetitions vary by leg. Public prompt matrices lack reruns, and their many model settings share examples. Original-template status in Mizrahi supplies a natural reference but not proof it was the best deployment default. The SWE-bench pairs compare complete systems with different attempt budgets. Nominal e-process guarantees require assumptions about future paired tasks and fixed candidate selection that historical benchmark matrices may not meet. Our protocols separate prospective live use, retrospective conditional checks, and fixed-matrix replays rather than pooling them into a single error-rate claim.

\section{Conclusion}
Incumbent-relative monitoring yields three useful answers: a candidate pool has evidence of an improvement, it lacks a gain as large as a chosen practical margin, or the task budget is insufficient. In our replays all three occur. Best-of-many selection inflates search-half gains in both prompt datasets, but the natural-incumbent paraphrase benchmark also contains substantial held-out improvements. The choice of incumbent and its stability across sessions are therefore part of the statistical design, not merely reporting details.
